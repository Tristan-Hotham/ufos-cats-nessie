Lights in the sky, a black cat the size of a Labrador, a hump in Loch Ness. People in the UK report all three, and the reports have been collected. I set {{ufo.n_gb|int}} UFO reports, {{nessie.n_total|int}} Nessie sightings and the replies of {{bigcats.n_forces|int}} police forces about big cats against the ordinary things that might explain them. The ordinary things win.

After the ghosts and the ley lines, I wanted to finish the set. Haunted houses turned out to be mostly old castles and old pubs, and ley lines turned out to be what you get when you draw lines through a crowded landscape. This time it is the three great British mysteries of the living sky and countryside: UFOs, big cats and the Loch Ness Monster.

I am not going to argue about any single sighting. That argument never ends, because one report almost never holds enough to settle it. Instead I counted. If the reports come from ordinary things (people looking, the places they look, the seasons when they are outside, the newspapers that tell them what to look for, and the offices that write it all down) those things should leave fingerprints on where and when the reports turn up. I wrote down the tests before I looked at the answers.

UFOs: where the people are

The US National UFO Reporting Center takes reports from anyone, and a cleaned copy of its records holds {{ufo.n_all|int}} of them up to 2014. {{ufo.n_gb|int}} come from the UK, each pinned to the town the witness named. I counted them by council. London's {{ufo.london_n|int}} reports count as one place, because most of them sit on one central London point.

The first thing that predicts reports is people. Double a council's population and its reports roughly double too. The scaling is {{ufo.x.elast|f2}}, where 1 would be exactly in step.

{{IMG|b_people.png|Scatter chart of {{ufo.n_units|int}} councils on log scales, with UFO reports rising in a straight line with population and Greater London at the top right|Each dot is a council. Reports rise in step with population.}}

Then I tried the usual suspects. Dark skies should let people see more. Air bases and airports put more lights in the sky. Sunny places should have more clear nights. If any of these mattered, councils with more of them should have more reports for their size. None of them does. Darker skies, a military airfield nearby, a civil airport nearby, more sunshine: every one comes out at about no difference, and the uncertainty rules out anything more than a modest effect.

{{IMG|b_map.png|Map of UK councils shaded by UFO reports per 100,000 people, with military airfields as triangles and civil airports as circles, and no clustering of high rates around the airfields|UFO reports per 100,000 people, with military airfields and civil airports.}}

I checked it two more ways. Half the reports come from within {{ufo.point.med_rep|f0}} km of a military airfield. For random spots spread out like the population, the figure is {{ufo.point.med_rnd|f0}} km. And when I scattered the same number of pretend air bases at random across the map, 500 times, the real ones did no better than the fakes.

{{IMG|b_checks.png|Four panels of dots with error bars, all clustered around 1, showing no effect of darker skies, military airfields, civil airports or sunshine under any of the checks|Every check, for each of the four suspects. 1 means no difference.}}

There is one striking thing in time. UK reports peaked in {{ufo.time.peak_year|raw}} with {{ufo.time.n_2009|int}}, and {{ufo.fireball_2009|int}} of them were fireballs. The National Archives' guide to the Ministry of Defence UFO files says that many of the ministry's own reports in 2008 and 2009 were Chinese lanterns. The ministry closed its UFO desk that December, and UK reports to the American center then fell to {{ufo.time.n_2013|int}} by 2013. It is tempting to join those dots, but the same simple model also finds significant drops in years when nothing happened, so I leave it as a description.

{{IMG|b_years.png|Bar chart of UK UFO reports by year from 1990 to 2013, rising to a peak of {{ufo.time.n_2009|int}} in 2009 with many fireballs, then falling|UK reports to NUFORC by year. 2014 is incomplete.}}

Nessie: born in the newspapers

The Official Loch Ness Monster Sightings Register lists every sighting it knows of, from Saint Columba in 565 onwards: {{nessie.site_count|int}} by its own count. Up to 1985 it reproduces the list in Henry Bauer's book The Enigma of Loch Ness, as scanned pages. I read the scans by machine and the later pages as text and got back {{nessie.n_total|int}} sightings, {{nessie.recovered_pc|f0}}% of the register's count.

Before 1933 there are only a few a decade. In 1933 a new road had just been built along the loch and the Inverness Courier ran a story about a "monster". That year has {{nessie.years.n_1933|int}} sightings. 1934, the year of the famous surgeon's photograph (a hoax, confessed in the 1990s), has {{nessie.years.n_1934|int}}, more than any year before or since. Later publicity did nothing like it. The sonar sweep of Operation Deepscan in 1987 and the Quest weekend in 2023, billed as the biggest search in fifty years, came in years with no more sightings than the five before.

{{IMG|b_nessie_years.png|Bar chart of Loch Ness sightings by year from 1900 to 2026, nearly empty before 1933, spiking to {{nessie.years.n_1933|int}} in 1933 and {{nessie.years.n_1934|int}} in 1934, then a steady handful a year with no rise in 1987 or 2023|Sightings in the register by year.}}

Nessie also keeps tourist hours. Per day, August has {{nessie.x.aug_jan|f0}} times as many sightings as January, and June to August hold {{nessie.month.all.summer_pc|f0}}% of all sightings with a known month. Through the year, sightings track the length of the day closely (a rank correlation of {{nessie.month.all.rho_daylight|f2}}), in Bauer's old list and in the register since 1986 alike. And where the register names a place, {{nessie.x.places_castle_bay_pc|f0}}% of recent sightings are at Urquhart Castle or in Urquhart Bay, which is where the visitors stand.

{{IMG|b_nessie_months.png|Bar chart of Loch Ness sightings per day by month, high in summer and low in winter, with a line for hours of daylight that rises and falls with the bars|Sightings per day of each month, against hours of daylight at the loch.}}

Big cats: lost in the filing

In 2019 someone used the Freedom of Information site WhatDoTheyKnow to ask every UK police force for a list of its "big cat" sightings since 2014. The replies are public, and they are a small study of their own. {{bigcats.released|int}} of {{bigcats.n_forces|int}} forces sent a list and {{bigcats.nil|word}} sent a nil return. {{bigcats.refused|int}} refused because searching would cost too much, and {{bigcats.not_held|int}} said they keep no such record. Surrey said a word search found more than 2,000 incidents, North Wales 1,160 and South Wales 596. Dyfed-Powys said it would have to read 65,295 calls about animals and suspicious behaviour. Forces explained that such searches also catch Puma trainers, Ford Pumas and leopard print.

The forces that did count found {{bigcats.records|int}} reports between them, from none at all in {{bigcats.nil|word}} forces to {{bigcats.herts_n|int}} in Hertfordshire. I had planned to test whether rural forces get more reports per head. Counting every force that gave a number, there is no clear pattern. Leave out the {{bigcats.nil|word}} forces that found nothing, and the more crowded forces have fewer reports per head, but with only {{bigcats.no_nil.n|int}} forces that is a weak pattern at best. So it depends on whether "nothing found" means nothing reported or a search that missed them, and every force searched in its own way. The thing these records show best is how the police file them.

What it adds up to

Three very different mysteries, one shape. Reports turn up where people are, when they are looking, after they have been told what to look for, and where someone writes them down. That does not tell you what anyone saw on any given night. It does mean that "there are more sightings here" and "there were lots of sightings then" are exactly what you would expect if nothing unusual were there at all.

How I did this

UFO reports are the National UFO Reporting Center's, as cleaned and geocoded by the planetsig/ufo-reports project, using only dates, shapes and places, never the witnesses' words. Population and sky darkness come from my Darkest skies study, sunshine from the Met Office's HadUK-Grid via Sun and rain, and airfields from OurAirports. The model is a negative binomial count model, run {{ufo.x.n_robust|int}} other ways as checks. The Loch Ness sightings are from the register's pages and the scanned Bauer list; the big cat counts are from the WhatDoTheyKnow replies and their attachments. No witness names or descriptions are reproduced anywhere. The paper has the full plan, every model and every check.

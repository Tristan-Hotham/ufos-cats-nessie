# Cover note: UFOs, Big Cats and Nessie

## Suitable journals (no fee to publish)

Checked online in 2026. Only routes with no article processing charge are listed.

1. **Folklore** (Taylor and Francis, for The Folklore Society). Peer reviewed (double anonymous). Subscription route: no charge to publish; open access would be optional and paid, so do not choose it. Length: the Duotrope listing gives articles of up to 12,000 words; the Taylor and Francis author instructions page could not be loaded from the workspace, so confirm the limit there before submitting. The paper (about 7,800 words before the references) fits. It publishes historical and cultural work on belief traditions, including legend and contemporary legend; the quantitative methods would need a short, plain explanation for its readers.
2. **Significance** (Royal Statistical Society and American Statistical Association). A magazine, not a peer-reviewed journal. Its contributor guidelines (April 2024) ask for feature articles of 1,000 to 3,500 words and mention no author fees. It would suit a shortened version built around the three main results and three or four figures.

Not recommended: the Journal of Scientific Exploration charges an article processing charge (USD 250, or 150 for members of its society), so it is not a no-fee route. Skeptical Inquirer's author guidelines could not be checked from the workspace.

The paper's text before the references is about 7,800 words, including tables and captions.

## Summary of the contribution (150 words)

Reports of UFOs, big cats and the Loch Ness Monster are usually argued about one case at a time. This paper treats three British report series as data. It tests, against a plan written before the outcomes were tabulated, whether ordinary features of observers, places, seasons and institutions explain where and when reports arise. For 1,905 UK reports to the National UFO Reporting Center, reports scale with council population (elasticity close to 1). Sky darkness, military airfields, civil airports and sunshine add nothing, and a placebo with random pseudo-bases and a point-level test agree. For 1,145 sightings recovered from the Official Loch Ness Monster Sightings Register, including Bauer's scanned list, sightings jump in 1933 and 1934, when the press created the story, but not around later searches. They follow day length through the year. For big cats, replies from 44 police forces show that only 20 could list their reports. The recording system is the finding.
